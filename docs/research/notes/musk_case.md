# Elon Musk's case for Mars — sources for the wiki chapter `musk` (checked up to 16 Oct 2026)

What the wiki's "Elon Musk's case for Mars" chapter rests on: each point with a short summary, at
most one verified quote with its event and date, and the key details. Unverified items and places
where sources disagree are listed at the end. Quotes used in the wiki are marked **(wiki)** — keep
them verbatim.

## 1. Core argument: insurance against extinction
- A species that lives on one planet will eventually face an extinction event, so life must become
  "multiplanetary", with a second home that can survive on its own. He frames the goal as
  preserving consciousness, which he thinks may be rare or even unique to us.
- **(wiki)** "One path is we stay on Earth forever, and then there will be some eventual extinction
  event." — International Astronautical Congress (IAC), Guadalajara, 27 Sept 2016, as published in
  *New Space* 5(2), June 2017.
- His own phrasings, verified and dated: "light of consciousness" (Starship update, 28 Sept 2019;
  Davos, 22 Jan 2026); "all our eggs, literally and metabolically, on one planet" (X, 7 Sept 2024);
  **(wiki)** "life insurance for life collectively" (Fox News, 5 May 2025).
- SpaceX's IPO filing (Form S-1, May 2026) gives the mission as "to make life multiplanetary, to
  understand the true nature of the universe, and to extend the light of consciousness to the
  stars"; the benefit is "species-level redundancy": "We do not want humans to have the same fate
  as dinosaurs." spacex.com: founded **(wiki)** "with the ultimate goal of enabling people to live
  on other planets".

## 2. Why Mars, and not the Moon or elsewhere
- 2016: about 1.5 times Earth's distance from the Sun, so still "decent sunlight"; "a little cold,
  but we can warm it up"; a CO2 atmosphere plants could use; water ice "almost everywhere" — with
  the CO2, methane and oxygen propellant (Sabatier) from a large solar field; gravity ~37–38 %, a
  day close to Earth's. Venus **(wiki)** a "hot acid bath", Mercury too close to the Sun.
- The Moon (2016): "nothing against" it, but small and airless — Mars "far better-suited" to grow
  into a self-sustaining civilisation. "It is not as resource-rich as Mars. It has got a 28-day
  day, whereas the Mars day is 24.5 hours." (IAC 2016 / *New Space* 2017.)
- SXSW, 11 Mar 2018: Mars is "far enough away from Earth" that a base there is more likely to
  survive than a Moon base.
- His view of the Moon has swung: 2017 "Moon Base Alpha"; Jan 2025 "a distraction"; 8 Feb 2026 the
  Moon first, faster to iterate (launches about every 10 days and a ~2-day trip, against Mars
  windows every 26 months and a ~6-month trip); 9 Feb 2026 (X) ships would still fly straight from
  Earth to Mars; May 2026 the S-1 calls a lunar presence a "stepping stone" to a city on Mars.

## 3. While we can
- After ~4.5 billion years this is the first time life on Earth can spread to another planet; the
  technology might not last (a World War III, a new "dark ages", civilisation dying "with a bang or
  a whimper").
- **(wiki)** "I think the wise move is to make life multiplanetary while we can." — American
  Geophysical Union Fall Meeting, San Francisco, 15 Dec 2015 (as reported by Space.com). Also GQ
  (Dec 2015), SXSW 2018 ("a seed of human civilization"), Starship update 28 Sept 2019 (the window
  "might not stay open for long"), *The Verdict* podcast (19 Mar 2025).

## 4. Something to look forward to
- TED (Apr 2017): a future without humans among the stars would be "incredibly depressing". IAC 2017:
  becoming spacefaring is "about believing in the future" (SpaceX's Mars page quotes it).
- **(wiki)** "[But] life cannot just be about solving one miserable problem after another. There need
  to be things that inspire you, that make you glad to wake up in the morning and be part of
  humanity." — SXSW, Austin, 11 Mar 2018 (Entrepreneur transcript). "miserable" is the verified
  word; some quote collections say "sad".

## 5. The self-sustaining threshold and the economics
- The test: the city keeps growing even if the ships from Earth stop coming (Mars Society, Oct 2020,
  "acid test", wording varies between transcripts; *The Verdict*, Mar 2025, "the critical
  civilizational threshold").
- People: ~1 million (2016; "on the order of a million people", Mar 2025; SpaceX's page: "upwards of
  one million people and millions of tonnes of cargo"). Cargo: "maybe a million tons" (Mar 2025),
  "about a million tons, but it might be 10 million tons" (May 2025). Ships: ~1,000 (2016); 100 a
  year (Jan 2020); "1,000 or 2,000 ships per Mars rendezvous" (2025, via Aerospace America).
- Economics (2016): Apollo-style ~$10 billion per person; the cost must improve by "five million
  percent"; four levers — full reusability (the biggest share), refilling in orbit, propellant made
  on Mars, the right propellant (methane); a target ticket under ~$200,000 (a median US house).
  Today's price list: Mars-surface cargo "no earlier than 2028, at a rate of $100 million per metric
  ton" (not comparable with the 2016 long-run estimate of ~$140,000 per tonne).

## 6. Timelines and how they moved
- 2016: crew "might be in the 10-year timeframe" if things go "super-well" (the paper); "2024 launch
  / 2025 arrival" is TechCrunch's summary of the Code Conference, not his words.
- 2017 (IAC Adelaide): two cargo ships in 2022 ("aspirational"), crew in 2024.
- 1 Dec 2020: "highly confident" of humans on Mars around 2026, maybe 2024 "if we get lucky".
- 7 Sept 2024 (X): uncrewed Starships in the 2026 window, crew two years later if the landings go
  well. 15 Mar 2025 (X): Starship to leave at the end of 2026 "carrying Optimus"; human landings
  "as soon as 2029, although 2031 is more likely". 29 May 2025: "50-50" for 2026.
- Feb 2026: SpaceX told investors it would do the Moon first (WSJ, 6 Feb); 8 Feb (X): a
  "self-growing city" on the Moon in under 10 years, a Mars city 20+ years, SpaceX to "begin doing
  so in about 5 to 7 years"; "the overriding priority is securing the future of civilization and
  the Moon is faster".
- Oct 2026: the late-2026 Mars window is "off the table" — orbital propellant transfer is still not
  demonstrated (Space.com, 14 Oct 2026); SpaceX lists Mars cargo "no earlier than 2028"; no firm
  date for a first crew since Feb 2026.
- **(wiki)** "My timelines historically have been optimistic." — SXSW 2018.

## 7. Counterpoints, one line each
- Radiation: Curiosity's measurements put the cruise there and back alone at ~0.66 Sv (Zeitlin et
  al., *Science*, May 2013), above NASA's 600 mSv career limit (NASA-STD-3001).
- Bodies: the effects of 0.38 g on pregnancy and children are unknown (Weinersmith & Weinersmith,
  *A City on Mars*, 2023).
- "Fix Earth first": astronomer Lucianne Walkowicz (TED 2015) called it hubris to think colonising
  another planet alone will save us. The "backup planet" framing mostly comes from critics — no
  dated primary source has Musk himself saying "backup".
- Planetary protection: no COSPAR-compliant way yet to send humans; Earth microbes could complicate
  the search for Martian life (Planetary Society, Aug 2021).
- Money: SpaceX's own S-1 warns its Moon/Mars plans rely on "unproven technologies" and "may not
  achieve commercial viability".
- "We can warm it up": a 2018 NASA-funded study found too little accessible CO2 to warm Mars with
  present-day technology (Jakosky & Edwards, *Nature Astronomy*).

## Flags
- The 2016 and 2017 papers are edited transcripts; the 2016 article is paywalled and was read from
  a third-party PDF whose title, volume and DOI match. Its "about 37 %" gravity and "24.5 hours" day
  are his round numbers (the real values ~0.38 g, 24 h 39 min).
- IAC 2017 is 29 Sept in Adelaide (the journal's note says 28 Sept).
- Second-hand: "I will not forget about Mars" (Benzinga); "1,000 or 2,000 ships" (Aerospace America);
  the 1-million-inhabitant pay condition (Fortune / Telegraph reading of the S-1); X posts quoted
  through news reports.
- Self-sufficiency estimates conflict across years: 40–100 years (2016), ~20 years (2021, 2024),
  "20+ years" (2026).
